What a weekly review should produce

A weekly review is a decision session. You collect loose commitments, look at what is still active and choose a manageable set of next actions. The output is a list you trust enough to leave alone while you work. An empty inbox is useful, but it is not the goal if the rest of your commitments remain unrealistic.

For a first review, choose one place for capture and gather your existing lists. Set aside a bounded session you can repeat. If you run out of time, finish by selecting tomorrow’s first action rather than spending another hour perfecting the system.

1. Collect before you organize

Check the places where work arrives: notes, messages, meeting records, email and unfinished AI conversations. Capture only the commitments you intend to consider. A useful article can stay in a reading list; it does not need to become a task called “Read article” unless you intend to act on it.

Record enough context to recognize the item next week. “Invoice” is ambiguous. “Send September invoice to Acme; confirm purchase order first” tells you what the action is and what might block it. Avoid assigning dates during collection unless the source contains a real deadline.

2. Decide what each unfinished item means

For each task, choose one outcome: keep it active, break it down, wait on someone, defer it or remove it. If the task is really an outcome, identify the next action. “Launch portfolio” might become “Choose three projects for the homepage.” You should be able to start the action without another planning meeting with yourself.

Separate waiting from doing. “Receive design approval” depends on someone else; your action might be “Send the mockup for approval.” Keep a review point for waiting items rather than treating them as tasks you failed to finish.

3. Check capacity before adding dates

Look at fixed commitments first. A week with travel, appointments and a delivery deadline has less room than an empty calendar suggests. Pick the work that fits the available attention, including interruptions. Leave undated work in its project list unless a date helps you make a decision.

For example, choose “Draft proposal outline” for Tuesday and leave “Improve website” unscheduled until it has a concrete next step. In Todoer, Today and Upcoming help inspect dated commitments; project lists preserve the work that has not earned a date yet.

Use AI to challenge the plan, not invent commitments

AI can help turn a messy list into questions you should answer. Give it a deliberately selected set of tasks, explain your available time and ask it to flag ambiguity or overload. It cannot know your real capacity, relationships or obligations unless you supply that context. A polished plan can still be an impossible plan.

Try: “Review these tasks for ambiguity and dependencies. I have two focused work sessions this week. Suggest three priorities and explain what you would defer. Do not create, move or date any tasks.” Review the suggestions before changing your actual list. Avoid sending sensitive work to a provider you have not approved.

A worked example: too many active projects

Suppose your lists contain a portfolio refresh, a client proposal, tax paperwork and a course you want to take. The proposal has a genuine deadline; tax paperwork requires one missing document. The portfolio matters but can wait. The course is an interest, not an obligation.

The review produces three actions: draft the proposal outline, request the missing document and choose a review date for the portfolio. Move the course to a someday list. You have not become less ambitious. You have stopped presenting every possible future as this week’s assignment.

When you miss a review

Resume from the current state. Do not reconstruct every day you missed or keep moving all overdue tasks to today. First identify deadlines that still matter, then remove stale commitments, then pick one action. A recovery review should make the backlog easier to face.

A useful stopping rule is simple: you know what matters next, you know what is waiting and the remaining items have a place. Once those conditions hold, stop organizing and begin. Read the capture inbox guide if loose items keep overwhelming the review.
